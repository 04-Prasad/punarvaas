The three questions this report answers

  1. Where is it unsafe, and for exactly whom? Every 10-resolution hex cell carries an annual probability of at least one damaging event from all perils combined, and a population count disaggregated from Census village totals by building footprint — so risk is reported for parts of a village, not for the village as a whole.
  2. Which land could receive people, and how many will it hold? Candidate sites are built from the same cells, screened through nine hard constraints, ranked, and then sized by the scarcest resource they have — with that resource named.
  3. Who moves first, and what does it cost? Each habitation gets a tier, a recommended action, a benefit-cost comparison between moving and protecting in place, and a funding phase under the district's annual budget.

4Priority, recommended action and the reason for it

Four tiers. The tier is not a score threshold: it is the output of a decision rule that looks at whether the hazard is active (subsidence and erosion do not wait for a trigger), whether a receiving site actually exists with capacity for the people, and whether protecting in place buys more safety per rupee than moving.

IMMEDIATE
Relocate within the current financial year; hazard is active or land loss is imminent
SHORT TERM
Relocate within 1–3 years; high risk with a viable site identified
MEDIUM TERM
Plan relocation over 3–7 years; risk significant, site requires development
MITIGATE
Protect in place; mitigation is more effective per rupee than moving people

6How sites were chosen

Two decisions had to be made explicit and defensible: which land is permissible, and among permissible land, which is preferable. They are handled separately and deliberately, because conflating them is how a ranking ends up recommending forest land with an excellent score.

Permissible — nine hard constraints, applied in order

The order matters. A cell is reported against the first constraint it fails, so cheap and definitive tests come first and judgement calls come last. That way "rejected: inside a RED zone" is never displaced by "rejected: too far".

  1. Zone RED — inside a red zone declared by the district ruleset.
  2. Zone ORANGE — not permitted for new residential layout.
  3. Residual hazard — combined annual probability above the resettlement cap. A site is never risk-free; the cap states how much residual risk the state is prepared to accept, and it is printed on every funnel above.
  4. Slope — above the NDMA habitable-slope norm (15°).
  5. Erosion — land predicted lost inside the planning horizon. Moving a village onto a retreating shoreline is a 20-year problem, not a solution.
  6. Inundation — below the flood or surge screening level.
  7. Land class — forest, CRZ or private land not readily available.
  8. Already built — occupied at settlement density.
  9. Distance — beyond what households accept from their origin. Livelihood, school and kinship ties are why relocation colonies stand empty when this is ignored.

Surviving cells are grouped into contiguous parcels by hex adjacency before ranking, because a site is a parcel: you cannot lay a road, a water line and a school across cells that do not touch.

Preferable — AHP weights, then TOPSIS ranking

CR ≤ 0.10 ⇒ judgements are internally consistent

Why fixed weights rather than a learned model There is no labelled corpus of "correct" resettlement sites in India to train on — the historical record contains sites chosen for reasons no one wrote down, including some that failed. So the weights come from a structured expert judgement whose internal consistency can be tested and whose numbers a District Magistrate can change and re-run. A learned weighting would be more impressive and less accountable.

Acceptance rules

  • A parcel is accepted only above a TOPSIS closeness of 0.35 — a site cannot merely be the best of a bad set.
  • It must hold at least 10 households; below that the per-household cost of a road, a water scheme and a school is indefensible.
  • A portfolio is accepted only if it covers at least 50% of the people at risk. Partial coverage is reported as a shortfall rather than hidden.

7Phasing under the budget

Habitations are funded tier-first, and within a tier by an exact 0/1 knapsack over avoided loss subject to the annual budget of ₹300.0 crore. Greedy benefit-per-rupee selection was rejected because it reliably funds four cheap medium-risk habitations ahead of one expensive IMMEDIATE one — arithmetically efficient and indefensible in front of the people left behind.

9What this analysis cannot do

A screening tool that presents itself as a survey gets people killed. The following are limits of method, not bugs, and they are stated here so that a reader can decide what weight to put on each finding.

It is a screening, not a geotechnical investigation.
Nothing here substitutes for borehole logs, a slope-stability analysis or a site inspection. The output identifies where to send an engineer, in what order.
Population is disaggregated, not counted.
Census village totals are redistributed within the village by building footprint area, storey estimate and probability of residential use, and the village total is conserved exactly. A cell-level count is therefore an expectation, not a roll of residents. Village totals are as good as the Census; cell values are as good as the footprint layer.
Land availability is the weakest input.
Ownership and land class drive the largest single exclusion in most funnels above, and it is the layer most likely to be a stand-in. A site on land that turns out to be private or forest is not a site. Revenue-record verification must precede any acquisition step.
Hazard interaction is approximated.
Perils are combined as independent annual probabilities with a coupling term for known cascades. Real cascades — a landslide-dammed river breaching, a cyclone on a saturated catchment — are correlated in ways a product of marginals understates. Where a coupling term applies it is reported per cell.
No value is placed on human life.
Every benefit-cost ratio in this report counts avoided physical damage only. Mortality is excluded deliberately: a value of statistical life makes relocation look profitable for reasons the state should decide politically, not arithmetically. Every BCR here is therefore a lower bound on the case for acting.
Effectiveness is never assumed complete.
No mitigation option is credited with eliminating risk, and no relocation is credited with zero residual hazard — the receiving site's own residual probability is carried through and printed.
Consent is not modelled.
Distance caps stand in for a social process they cannot replace. Households refuse to move for reasons — grazing rights, a shrine, a fishing landing, a school-going child — that no raster contains. Gram Sabha consultation is a precondition, not a downstream step.
This snapshot contains synthetic inputs.
Marked row by row in section 8. Figures are internally consistent and externally unverified. Publishing any habitation-level number from this snapshot as fact would be a misrepresentation.

What would change these answers

  • A real ownership and land-class layer would move sites in or out of the funnel at gate 7 — the single highest-leverage data substitution available.
  • The GSI landslide inventory would replace the trained susceptibility surface and change cell-level probabilities, though the ranking of habitations is driven mainly by subsidence and slope, which are observed.
  • CGWB block groundwater status and the JJM source register would replace the inferred water yield, which is the binding constraint on 108 of 108 candidate parcels here — so it directly sets capacity.
  • A district-agreed residual-hazard cap and travel-distance limit would replace the defaults used above. Both are policy choices, both are printed, and both can be changed and re-run in under a second per district.
